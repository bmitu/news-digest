Project Suncatcher TPU satellite

3 sources · tracked since Sep 27 · 1 new article today · 2 independent origins: Ars Technica, Google

Google is preparing to launch its first Project Suncatcher test satellite, called MVP, on October 1, carrying four of its TPU AI accelerators, according to Ars Technica.12 The satellite is a Planet prototype that will fly on SpaceX's Transporter-18 rideshare.345 Ars Technica reports the spacecraft is about the size of a refrigerator, and that it comes from Planet Labs, which had already built it for an early test, rather than being built by Google from scratch.678

Google plans to run Gemini models on the TPUs for testing, and the satellite is expected to operate for just a few months.910 Ars Technica says the solar panels supply only about one kilowatt of power, citing The New York Times, and separately says the cooling system can only operate in brief spurts of about 15 minutes before the TPUs must shut down.1112

Ars Technica says cooling the chips may be the biggest issue, something many observers have pointed out, and that Google's solution uses thermal interface material connecting the chips to aluminum and copper heat pipes that conduct heat into a radiator.1314 Import AI reports that Google has stress-tested the TPUs for the g-forces of launch, and has tested radiation response, finding its Trillium TPUs "hold up remarkably well, and can survive a radiation total ionizing dose greater than what they would receive during a five-year space mission".1516

Ars Technica says the original plan was to launch two custom satellites in 2027; those launches are still planned, but the team expects it will be years before Suncatcher moves from "project" to "product."1718 Import AI's author is enthusiastic, saying it seems very likely that humanity will move a very large amount of computation into orbit quickly, while also calling cooling a likely challenge.1920

Why is it so hard to get rid of heat from computer chips in space, and why does the radiator need time to catch up?

Chips give off a huge amount of heat, and getting rid of it in the vacuum of space is very difficult, according to Import AI.1 Ars Technica reports that radiators already used in space are built to remove only a relatively small amount of heat, while AI accelerators produce far more.2 Google's design carries heat from the chips through a thermal interface material and aluminum and copper heat pipes into a radiator that projects it into space.34 The radiator needs time to catch up because the cooling system can only run in short bursts of about 15 minutes, after which the TPUs must shut down while the radiators work through the heat.5
