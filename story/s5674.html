Thomas Nagel criticizes AI consciousness claims in Wired interview

Last scan Sat Nov 28, 17:01 UTC
2 sources · tracked since Nov 24 · single origin, unclear which

Philosopher Thomas Nagel, best known for a 1974 essay about bats, sharply criticized the idea of AI consciousness in a Wired interview that MIT Technology Review places in the week before its story, just after he won the $1 million Berggruen Prize.12 Aiming at people at Anthropic and elsewhere who care about AI model welfare, Nagel said that some of the world's smartest people were "being completely idiotic."3 He argued that supposing AI might be conscious is "ridiculous" because such systems only produce language, which he says does not on its own naturally manifest consciousness.45 MIT Technology Review summarizes his broader view as holding that similarities in information processing cannot justify ignoring the physical differences between biological brains and silicon-based AI.67

A screenshot of one of Nagel's answers stirred philosophy professors on social media, where some called his certainty "terrible" and "bizarre" and others defended him as perhaps meaning that consciousness requires being alive, which AI is not.89 Andrew Y. Lee argued that Nagel's point is that physical implementation matters, not that biology is magic, but Robert Long replied that this reading was too charitable given that Nagel deemed it ridiculous even to take the question seriously.710 Zvi Mowshowitz argues that Nagel, who called part of Anthropic's approach to its AI "criminal," is flatly wrong about what instructions the models get and how those work, though Mowshowitz also cautions that the edited interview could have garbled Nagel's points. inference111213

Anthropic, which counts respected philosophers among its supporters, had named possible AI consciousness or sentience as an investor risk before going public, saying its approach might lead it to adopt more rules and restrictions than competitors. inference141516 MIT Technology Review also notes that one recent poll found 33% of the US public expects AI to definitely or probably become conscious and 7% say it already has, while 39% of 1,092 philosophers surveyed in 2020 said some future AI systems will be conscious.1718
