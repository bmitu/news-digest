Tech companies create AI standards body

Last scan Sat Oct 3, 04:20 UTC
2 sources · tracked since Sep 29 · single origin (The Washington Sun)

Google, OpenAI and Anthropic are reportedly working jointly on a plan to create their own AI safety-focused "standards body."1 Zvi Mowshowitz describes as welcome news that the three companies are planning to form the new body by early in 2027, tentatively titled the Standards Authority for Frontier AI or SAFA.2

Some AI safety experts have rejected the model as effectively allowing the firms to police themselves, according to the Washington Sun.3 The Washington Sun reports that one alternative is a government tax on AI companies to fund safety operations, to ensure independence and keep the financial burden off taxpayers.4 Separately, Elon Musk has suggested that frontier labs should review each other's models before release without government oversight.5

What minimum requirements do Hinton, Russell and Narayanan say evaluators must meet to be credibly independent of the AI companies?

Geoffrey Hinton, Stuart Russell and Arvind Narayanan set out minimum standards for credible embedded evaluators in a public letter, starting with the requirement that frontier AI companies rely on evaluators that are meaningfully independent.123 On financial and commercial ties, they say payments cannot depend on findings, there should be no ownership, governing or other commercial relationship, and the company should have no editorial control.4 They also call for any conflicts of interest to be disclosed, for evaluators to be transparent, and for companies to incorporate differing viewpoints and areas of expertise.5 Finally, they say evaluators should be shielded from retaliation by the companies they embed with, and should get access equivalent to the companies' own highly privileged employees, with exceptions for protecting data.67

Why might the companies' antitrust and legal exposure complicate cooperating on safety, and how could insurance get around it?

MIRI's Nate Soares says it is unclear whether AI companies can legally coordinate to slow down development because of antitrust law.1 Zvi Mowshowitz says the companies are asking for targeted antitrust relief specifically so they can collaborate on safety standards and slow development.2 One workaround, suggested by Lawfare, is to mutualize risk through insurance, and the mutual could then enforce safety, auditing and related standards as conditions of its policies.34 Mowshowitz cautions, however, that existential and the worst catastrophic risks cannot really be insured, but says insurance could still be a way to implement wise interventions.5
