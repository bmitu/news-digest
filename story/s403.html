Nature: Stratego Superhuman AI

Last scan Mon Oct 5, 14:51 UTC
3 sources · 288 points on Hacker News · tracked since Oct 1 · 2 independent origins: Ars Technica, Ataraxos team

A Nature paper was flagged (in a Latent Space newsletter) as presenting the first superhuman Stratego AI, built on RL and test-time compute under imperfect information.1 The AI, called Ataraxos, was built by a team of researchers from Carnegie Mellon, MIT, New York University and Stanford, and Ars Technica reports it beat Pim Niemeijer, arguably the best Stratego player of all time, 15 games to one with four draws.234 Niemeijer played 20 online games over three weeks, and he won just once; at the 2025 Stratego World Championship, the AI won 38 of 40 games against attendees.56

Ataraxos learned by self-play over 163 million games, and its key innovation over DeepMind's DeepNash was thinking ahead before each move.789 The team made big, bold changes in strategy early in training and small, careful ones later, because hidden information tends to send self-play learning algorithms around in circles.10

Ataraxos reportedly needed 16 GPUs for a week plus four GPUs for four days, whereas DeepNash was trained for two to three months on 1,024 chips, a run the team estimates would cost $3 million to $4.5 million at 2025 prices.1112 It played about 34 times fewer games than DeepNash, and the same approach beat three world champions at Barrage Stratego.131415

Researchers claim Niemeijer's single win was not really a flaw, and Gabriele Farina says that "Even a perfect strategy, sometimes it will just lose."1617 Farina says the bot has "skewed the metagame a little bit," and Ataraxos currently cannot explain its moves.1819

How many possible piece setups and how long can a Stratego game last, and why did that stump earlier AIs like DeepNash?

Stratego has more than a decillion possible piece setups, according to MIT computer scientist Gabriele Farina, who points to 40 pieces that could be in any order.1 Farina also says a game can easily last 2,000 moves, versus about 40 in chess.2 By contrast, Texas Hold'em has only 1,326 possible hands, few enough for a machine to weigh them all.3 The team explains that the balancing act of bluffing is what stumped earlier AIs like DeepMind's DeepNash (introduced in 2022): when players bluff too often, their threats mean nothing; when they never bluff, they become predictable.45 DeepMind couldn't make look-ahead search work in Stratego because the search space was too large, leaving it an open question whether it was worth trying.6 Ataraxos got around this with a belief model that guesses the opponent's hidden pieces and samples plausible arrangements instead of iterating through every one.7

What unusual strategies did Ataraxos use, such as bluffing its way back from a 2% win chance or hiding its flag behind two bombs?

Gabriele Farina says that for humans it is very hard, when you know a secret, to make decisions ignoring that you know it, while for machines it is easy. This, the team says, leads machines to make moves a human would only do while bluffing, and follow up on them much better than humans.12 While a human might try big gambles to come back from a significant deficit, Ataraxos would work its way back into the game slowly and methodically. When Ataraxos estimates its opponent has no reason to suspect a weak spot, it leaves that spot alone, even if it might look like a disaster waiting to happen to anyone who can see both sides of the board.34 Ataraxos cannot currently explain why it makes the moves it makes.5

How did the team get such fast training on GPUs, and how many moves per second does their simulator run?

Farina and lead author Samuel Sokota achieved this efficiency by writing a simulator that runs millions of moves per second on graphics cards. inference123 As a result, Ataraxos needed 16 GPUs for a week, plus four more GPUs for four days to train its belief model. DeepNash, by contrast, was trained for two to three months on 1,024 of Google's specialized chips. inference456 Farina notes that academic labs lack large GPU fleets, which is the constraint this efficiency worked around. inference7 The algorithm also learned with fewer games: it played about 34 times fewer than DeepNash.89

What real-world uses do the researchers see for this technique, such as war gaming, negotiations or financial markets?

The team has its sights set on scenarios far more complex than board or card games; the article contrasts these with real-world problems like negotiations, financial markets, or military conflicts, which usually lack fixed rules and clear winners.123 The Ataraxos team argues the gap between games and those messy problems is smaller than it looks, since tackling any real problem starts with building a simplified model of it.45 No specific application to negotiations or financial markets was described beyond naming them as the harder real-world settings.
