Nature: Stratego Superhuman AI

Last scan Sun Oct 4, 19:31 UTC
3 sources · 284 points on Hacker News · tracked since Oct 1 · 2 independent origins: Ars Technica, Ataraxos team

A Nature paper was flagged (in a Latent Space newsletter) as presenting the first superhuman Stratego AI, built on RL and test-time compute under imperfect information.1 The AI, called Ataraxos, was built by a team of researchers from Carnegie Mellon, MIT, New York University and Stanford, and Ars Technica reports it beat Pim Niemeijer, arguably the best Stratego player of all time, 15 games to one with four draws.234 Niemeijer played 20 online games over three weeks, and he won just once; at the 2025 Stratego World Championship, the AI won 38 of 40 games against attendees.56

Ataraxos learned by self-play over 163 million games, and its key innovation over DeepMind's DeepNash was thinking ahead before each move.789 The team made big, bold changes in strategy early in training and small, careful ones later, because hidden information tends to send self-play learning algorithms around in circles.10

Ataraxos reportedly needed 16 GPUs for a week plus four GPUs for four days, whereas DeepNash was trained for two to three months on 1,024 chips, a run the team estimates would cost $3 million to $4.5 million at 2025 prices.1112 It played about 34 times fewer games than DeepNash, and the same approach beat three world champions at Barrage Stratego.131415

Researchers claim Niemeijer's single win was not really a flaw, and Gabriele Farina says that "Even a perfect strategy, sometimes it will just lose."1617 Farina says the bot has "skewed the metagame a little bit," and Ataraxos currently cannot explain its moves.1819

How many possible piece setups and how long can a Stratego game run compared with chess or poker, and why does that make it so hard for AI?

Stratego has vastly more possible starting arrangements than the hidden information in poker: MIT computer scientist Gabriele Farina says its 40 pieces can be in any order, which is more than a decillion possible setups, versus just 1,326 possible hands in Texas Hold'em. inference12 Games also run far longer: Farina says chess usually lasts about 40 moves, while a Stratego game can easily last 2,000 moves.3 Researcher Eugene Vinitsky describes the combination as a massive amount of hidden information that unfolds over a very long time scale.45 Bluffing adds to the difficulty: players who bluff too often make their threats meaningless, while those who never bluff become predictable, and that balancing act is what stumped earlier AIs like DeepMind's DeepNash.678 DeepMind couldn't make look-ahead search work in Stratego because the search space was too large, leaving it an open question whether it was worth trying.9

How does Ataraxos's second neural network guess the identity of hidden enemy pieces, and how does that feed its look-ahead before each move?

Stratego hides the identity of each piece. An opponent knows where your pieces are but not what they are, and identities are revealed only when two pieces collide in battle.1 Ataraxos's second neural network is a "belief model" that is trained to guess the opponent's hidden pieces from how those pieces have been moving.2 Ataraxos does not run through every possible arrangement of hidden pieces. It samples plausible ones, plays out candidate moves in each, and picks a move based on how those playouts turned out.3 This look-ahead before each move is something the earlier DeepNash never had. DeepMind could not make a search work in Stratego because the search space was too large.45 The article does not detail how the belief model is built beyond its training on observed piece movement.
