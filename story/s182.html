U.S. appeals court upholds designation of Anthropic as supply chain risk

2 sources · 498 points on Hacker News · tracked since Sep 27 · 1 new article today · 2 independent origins: Ars Technica, CNBC

A federal appeals court panel in Washington, D.C., upheld the Pentagon's designation of Anthropic as a supply chain risk in a 2-1 decision on Friday, a blow to the AI company in its months-long fight with the Trump administration.123 Judge Gregory Katsas, joined by Judge Neomi Rao, wrote that the Department had ample support for concluding that continued integration of Claude into its information systems presented a statutorily covered national-security risk, and that the Secretary did not transgress any limits on his authority under the Supply Chain Security Act or the Constitution.45 The court majority said the Department reasonably feared that Anthropic might manipulate Claude's design to prevent it from performing national-security functions, and noted that restrictions had stopped Claude from performing tasks requested by government users.67

According to Ars Technica, the D.C. Circuit did not dispute the district court's finding that Anthropic acted with no bad motive, but said no bad motive is required under the broader definition in section 4713, which it reviewed.8 Judge Karen Henderson dissented, arguing that the statute's verbs are directed at deliberately impeding or eavesdropping and should not cover a contractor's honest and upfront enforcement of restrictions on use.910

The Pentagon relied on two distinct designations, so the case had to be litigated in two separate courts; a San Francisco federal judge ruled last month that one designation was illegal, while the D.C. appeals court upheld the second designation.111213

An Anthropic spokesperson said the company respectfully disagrees, noting another federal court held the parallel designation unlawful, and that it is considering all options, including further review.141516 CNBC reports the panel delayed the decision from taking immediate effect so Anthropic can seek a rehearing or en banc review, and Anthropic could also ask the Supreme Court to take the case.171819

Why did the D.C. appeals court review the blacklisting under a different law than the San Francisco judge did, and how does that change the outcome?

The Pentagon relied on two separate designations to justify its action, so they had to be litigated in two courts.1 The two courts were looking at different statutes: the San Francisco judge found the blacklisting violated 10 U.S.C. § 3252, while the D.C. Circuit reviewed it under 41 U.S.C. § 4713.23 The D.C. Circuit said the district court was tasked with reviewing whether the decision was allowed under one law, while the appeals court has exclusive jurisdiction to review it under a different, more permissive grant of authority; Congress gave it that exclusive jurisdiction over Section 4713 designations.456 The court reasoned that Section 4713 covers "any person," not just adversaries or foreign entities, and pointed to the word "deny," which it said applies to Anthropic preventing the US from using certain Claude features. It concluded that the Secretary's concern about Anthropic disabling Claude from performing lawful actions requested by the Department qualifies as a supply chain risk under Section 4713.78 Anthropic notes that another federal court has already held the parallel designation unlawful, and it is considering all options, including further review. Judge Henderson dissented, arguing the definition should not cover a contractor's honest and upfront enforcement of restrictions on its product's use.91011

What dispute led the Pentagon and Anthropic to fall out, and which uses of Claude did Anthropic refuse to allow?

The Pentagon and Anthropic fell out after talks over Claude's deployment on the military's GenAI.mil platform collapsed in September 2025, following a $200 million contract signed that July.1 The core dispute was that the Defense Department wanted unfettered access to Anthropic's models for all lawful purposes, while Anthropic wanted assurance its technology would not be used for fully autonomous weapons or domestic mass surveillance.2 Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth accused Anthropic of trying to seize veto power over the military's operational decisions.3 Anthropic alleged, and a California district judge agreed, that the administration retaliated after the company refused to drop restrictions on lethal autonomous warfare and mass surveillance of Americans.4

What does the ruling mean for defense contractors and federal agencies that use Claude, and when could the ban take effect?

Judge Gregory Katsas, writing for the majority, said the Department had ample support for its conclusion that the continued integration of Claude into its information systems, by the Department or its contractors, presented a statutorily covered national-security risk.12 Ars Technica reports that the government's action also ordered federal agencies to stop using Anthropic's products and banned defense contractors from doing any business with Anthropic.3 A San Francisco federal judge ruled last month that one of the two designations was illegal, and the D.C. Circuit ruling upheld the second designation.45 The ban will not take effect immediately, because the panel delayed its decision to give Anthropic time to seek a rehearing by the same panel or by the full D.C.
