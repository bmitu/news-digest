U.S. appeals court upholds designation of Anthropic as supply chain risk

Last scan Sat Oct 3, 04:20 UTC
3 sources · 499 points on Hacker News · tracked since Sep 27 · 1 new article on Oct 2 · 2 independent origins: Ars Technica, CNBC

Judge Gregory Katsas, writing for the majority with Judge Neomi Rao, said the Department had ample support for concluding that continued use of Claude in its systems presented a statutorily covered national-security risk.12 Ars Technica reports the court held that no bad motive is required under 41 U.S.C. § 4713, whose definition covers the risk that "any person" may manipulate covered technology, and said the Secretary's concern about Anthropic disabling Claude from lawful actions qualifies.345

The ruling does not conflict with an earlier California ruling: a Northern District of California judge ruled last month that the action was illegal under a different statute, and the appeals court said it had no quarrel with that court's conclusions on bad motive under section 3252 but that section 4713 is broader. inference67

Judge Karen Henderson dissented, arguing the statute's verbs are directed at deliberately impeding or eavesdropping and should not cover a contractor's honest and upfront enforcement of restrictions on use that the government disfavors.89

An Anthropic spokesperson said the company respectfully disagrees, noting another federal court held the parallel designation unlawful, and that it is considering all options including further review.101112 CNBC reports the panel delayed the decision's effect so Anthropic can seek rehearing or en banc review, and Anthropic could also ask the Supreme Court to take the case.1314 Ars Technica adds that Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick recently said the Trump administration and Anthropic have patched up their relationship and are "in tune."15

What did the Pentagon and Anthropic actually disagree over when contract talks collapsed, and how much was the original deal worth?

Contract talks collapsed over the scope of military access: the Pentagon wanted unfettered access to Anthropic's models for all lawful purposes.1 Anthropic, however, wanted assurance that its technology would not be used for fully autonomous weapons or domestic mass surveillance.2 Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth accused Anthropic of trying to "seize veto power over the operational decisions of the United States military."3 The original deal was a $200 million contract that Anthropic signed with the Pentagon in July 2025.4

Why are there two separate courts ruling on the same blacklisting, and which law did each one review?

Two courts are ruling because the Pentagon justified its supply chain risk action with two distinct designations, and each had to be litigated in a different court.1 The split also reflects jurisdiction: Congress gave the D.C. Circuit exclusive jurisdiction to review procurement actions taken under Section 4713 designations, while the district court was tasked with reviewing the decision under a different law.23 The D.C. Circuit reviewed it under 41 U.S.C. § 4713, which does not have the same restrictions, and a 2-1 panel upheld the designation.45 The appeals court did not dispute the lower court's finding that bad motive is required under section 3252 and that Anthropic showed none, but it held that section 4713 requires no such motive.67

What did the California judge say about the blacklisting, and did she find it violated the First Amendment?

Judge Rita Lin, the Biden-appointed judge in the Northern District of California, determined that the Pentagon's blacklisting of Anthropic violated the First Amendment.1 Lin wrote: "The empty invocation of national security is not a blank check to punish and retaliate against government critics."2 Anthropic alleged, and Lin agreed, that the Trump administration illegally retaliated against the company after it refused to drop restrictions on using its products for lethal autonomous warfare and mass surveillance of Americans.3 A judge in the Northern District of California ruled that the action was illegal because Anthropic does not meet the definition of a supply-chain risk, which is limited to "the risk that an adversary may sabotage, maliciously introduce unwanted function, or otherwise subvert… a covered system."45

What specific incidents did the appeals court cite as showing Claude's restrictions were a risk to the military?

The appeals court said Anthropic builds restrictions into Claude that stop the model from doing tasks Anthropic wants to prevent, and that on more than one occasion these restrictions stopped Claude from performing tasks government users requested.1 It also cited a recent dispute over whether contractual prohibitions barred the use of Claude in an ongoing overseas military operation, which left the Department uncertain whether Claude would perform as needed.2 The specific earlier occasions when Claude's restrictions blocked government tasks were not detailed.
