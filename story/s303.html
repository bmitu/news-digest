San Francisco court rules one DOD designation against Anthropic unlawful

2 sources · tracked since Sep 29 · 1 new today · 2 independent origins: Ars Technica, CNBC

The Department of Defense used two distinct designations, litigated in two courts, and a San Francisco federal judge ruled last month that one of them was illegal.12 Ars Technica reports that the Northern District of California judge, Rita Lin, found the action illegal because Anthropic does not meet the definition of a supply-chain risk under 10 U.S.C. § 3252, and also determined it violated the First Amendment.342

A federal appeals court panel in Washington, D.C., however, ruled 2-1 on Friday to uphold the Pentagon's blacklisting of Anthropic, rejecting the company's argument that the Defense Department's ban on its Claude models was arbitrary, unauthorized and unconstitutional.56 Judge Gregory Katsas wrote for the majority, joined by Judge Neomi Rao, that the Department had ample support for concluding that Claude's continued integration presented a statutorily covered national-security risk, while Judge Karen LeCraft Henderson dissented.78 According to Ars Technica, the D.C. Circuit did not dispute Lin's finding but said it reviewed the blacklisting under 41 U.S.C. § 4713, which it read as not requiring bad motive.910 Henderson disputed the majority's reading, arguing the statute's verbs are directed at deliberately impeding or eavesdropping and should not cover "a contractor’s honest and upfront enforcement of restrictions on a covered article’s use disfavored by the government."1112

The court wrote that the case raises "profoundly difficult questions," with the government raising the prospect of overly constrained AI models shutting down unexpectedly and Anthropic raising the prospect of unconstrained models hallucinating inappropriate targets.131415

Anthropic said in a statement that it respectfully disagrees, remains confident, and is considering all options, including further review.161718 CNBC reports the panel delayed the decision from taking immediate effect so Anthropic can seek rehearing or en banc review, and Anthropic could also ask the Supreme Court to take the case.1920 Ars Technica notes that Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick recently said the Trump administration and Anthropic have patched up their relationship and are "in tune."21

Why did the D.C. Circuit say it could uphold the blacklisting when the San Francisco judge found Anthropic isn't a supply-chain risk?

The D.C. Circuit did not dispute the district court's primary finding; it said the district court was tasked with reviewing whether the decision was allowed under one law, while the appeals court has exclusive jurisdiction to review it under a different, more permissive grant of authority.12 The appeals court reviewed the blacklisting under 41 U.S.C. § 4713, which lacks those restrictions, and Congress gave the D.C. Circuit exclusive jurisdiction over procurement actions under that provision.345 The court said it had no quarrel with the finding that Anthropic acted with no bad motive, but held that no bad motive is required under the broader Section 4713 definition.67 The majority reasoned that Section 4713's wording covers "any person," not just adversaries, and that its word "deny" applies to Anthropic preventing the U.S. from using certain Claude features.89 The majority concluded that the Department reasonably feared Anthropic might manipulate Claude's design to prevent it from performing national-security functions that the Department deems contractually authorized and necessary. Judge Henderson dissented, arguing the verbs in the definition are directed at deliberately impeding or eavesdropping, and that it should not be read to cover a contractor's honest and upfront enforcement of restrictions on a covered article's use disfavored by the government.1011

What led the Pentagon and Anthropic's talks to collapse, and what did each side want on autonomous weapons and mass surveillance?

Talks collapsed in September 2025, while Anthropic and the Pentagon were negotiating Claude's deployment on the DOD's GenAI.mil platform, under a $200 million contract signed that July.1 The Defense Department wanted unfettered access to Anthropic's models for all lawful purposes.2 Anthropic wanted assurance that Claude would not be used for fully autonomous weapons or domestic mass surveillance.3 The two sides could not agree. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth accused Anthropic of trying to "seize veto power over the operational decisions of the United States military."4 The court also said a dispute arose over whether Anthropic's contractual prohibitions barred Claude's use in an ongoing overseas military operation, leaving the Department uncertain whether Claude would perform as needed.56
