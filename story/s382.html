Protein watermarking research (SynthID Bio)

Last scan Fri Oct 2, 18:55 UTC
2 sources · tracked since Oct 1 · 2 independent origins: Ars Technica, Google DeepMind

Latent Space's AINews roundup, citing @demishassabis, reports that SynthID Bio, watermarking for AI-generated proteins, is published in Nature, with open-sourced tools.1 Google DeepMind published a research paper proposing protein watermarking, a system that marks AI-designed protein sequences without compromising their function so that proteins from trusted researchers can be identified.23 The problem it targets is that DNA-screening software does not pick out AI-designed proteins, since nobody has characterized them well enough to know whether they are threats.4

Ars Technica reports the team started with ProteinMPNN, with a SynthID variant (SynthIDBio) that uses a key and the identity of previously chosen amino acids to suggest a new one; ProteinMPNN then checks whether it works for forming a functional protein and rejects it if not, so watermark amino acids are only incorporated when consistent with a functional protein.56 Because the watermark is spread randomly along the protein, detection is not yes-or-no: one must scan the whole sequence with the key and measure how often the suggested amino acids appear.78

In tests, watermarked proteins designed to interact with key natural proteins bound their intended targets, which Ars Technica calls less rigorous than a catalyst test but suggestive of no serious problems in harder design tasks.910

Google envisions DNA synthesizers using keys from trusted organizations to quickly tell whether an unknown protein is a trusted AI design, letting them focus on untrusted ones, though this does not guarantee DNA order security.1112 The team itself highlights several weaknesses: security depends on how keys are distributed and maintained, very short proteins may carry too few watermark amino acids, and fusing the protein with an unwatermarked one might dilute the watermark.131415

Why was it uncertain that SynthID would work on proteins when it works on text and images?

Google's SynthID, which can add a subtle watermark to AI-generated digital material, influences the probability of certain choices the AI makes, and that bias ends up systematically distributed throughout the product, whether it's text or images, according to Ars Technica.1 Proteins, however, are also fragile: many have regions where only limited changes to the amino acid sequence are tolerable, and others where even a slight deviation inactivates the protein.2 As a result, it was unclear whether SynthID could hide enough signal in a typical protein, or whether packing in enough information for a working watermark would leave the proteins inactive. Ars Technica reports that the only way to find out was to try it.34

Who would hold and distribute the keys, and what stops them from leaking or being misused?

Google's proposal envisions DNA synthesis companies receiving sets of watermark keys from trusted organizations such as universities or major biotech companies.12 The watermark is built on a key, similar to a cryptographic key, and detecting it means scanning the whole sequence with that key in hand.34 The Google team itself acknowledges that the system is only as secure as the process used to distribute and maintain the keys, which is a weak point it lists among several holes.5 However, the reporting does not describe any specific mechanism that would stop keys from leaking or being misused, and it names no single body that would hold and distribute them.
