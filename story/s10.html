Claude discovers novel enzyme system

5 sources · 780 points on Hacker News · tracked since Sep 27 · 1 new article today · single origin (Anthropic)

Anthropic announced that Claude autonomously discovered a novel enzyme system associated with an array of DNA repeats, a pattern reminiscent of CRISPR, and says it doesn't yet know the system's function.12 Anthropic says this is the first result from its new molecular biology lab, where a team of Anthropic biologists is using Claude to explore and accelerate fundamental biology research.34

Anthropic says about 950 agents spent 21 hours and 210 million tokens, gathering over 200,000 reverse transcriptases, picking 3,500 candidate systems and narrowing them to 20 reports.567 Anthropic says early experiments show the ART array is expressed as distinct short RNAs, while further experiments are underway to learn how ART works.8910 Anthropic says the underlying reverse transcriptase had been identified before, but that Claude appears to be the first to notice the associated repeat array and accessory protein.11

Some biologists were angered by the claims; a viral post from one biologist, subsequently endorsed by the chair and CEO of Eli Lilly, said that finding a weird cluster of genes and repeats is often the easy part.121314 Mario Rodríguez Mestre of the University of Copenhagen said his team had already discovered this pattern and wondered whether Anthropic learned from his Claude conversations; Anthropic denies this.1516

Dario Amodei reportedly called the result PhD-worthy but of unclear significance, while @suchenzang questioned the agent-hour accounting and the lack of wet-lab detail.1718 MIT Technology Review's newsletter argues that whittling 200,000 candidates down to a few is legitimate scientific work, but that once the standard is whether Claude itself made a discovery, all of it becomes evidence in a debate with only two answers: breakthrough or bust.1920 Zvi Mowshowitz judges that as biology it is not a big deal, comparing it to something a PhD student might find.21

What did Anthropic's early experiments in E. coli show, and how much wet-lab work has actually been done on the system?

Anthropic says its first experiments found that the array of repeats next to the reverse transcriptase gene is expressed as a set of distinct short RNAs, hinting that something analogous to CRISPR may be at work.1 Latent Space's summary says the experiments were run by humans and involved expression in E. coli plus RNA-seq.2 Latent Space's summary says Anthropic explicitly states the system's biological function and any programmable editing utility remain unknown, and Anthropic says its work to understand the primary function of ARTs is ongoing.345 Latent Space's summary reports pushback: @suchenzang questioned the agent-hour accounting and the lack of wet-lab detail, and @iScienceLuvr said the lab work is "very limited", essentially confirming the system can be expressed.6

Why do critics say that spotting a repeat array and showing it makes short RNAs is not a big discovery?

A technical critic argued the work looks incomplete because identifying repeat arrays and showing they produce short RNAs is a fairly standard genomics workflow, with similar analyses already seen in systems such as VIPR.12 The same commenter noted that repeat arrays are already known to be interesting motifs, so the novelty would have to come from a new biological function or a substantially novel discovery process, and they felt neither was clearly established.34 Biologist Lucas Harrington made a related point in a post that was endorsed by the chair and CEO of Eli Lilly. He said that finding a weird cluster of genes and repeats is often the easy part, and that the hard part is figuring out what the system actually does.56 Anthropic itself says the system's function is still unknown, and other commenters noted there is no evidence yet of programmable gene editing.789

How much of the work did Claude do by itself, and how much did Anthropic's scientists do?

Anthropic says its scientists gave Claude a prompt and did the lab work, while Claude agents did the searching and analysis in between.1 According to Anthropic, about 950 Claude agents spent 21 hours and 210 million tokens searching before one flagged a repeating DNA pattern next to an odd-looking reverse transcriptase gene.23 Anthropic says the agents gathered over 200,000 reverse transcriptases, picked 3,500 candidate systems and narrowed them to 20 that were written up as reports, work it says would take an expert weeks to months.45 Humans then ran the experiments: the team's experiments showed the repeat array is expressed as distinct short RNAs, and Anthropic says its scientists review Claude's hypotheses and do all the lab work.67

How did Lucas Harrington, the biologist behind the viral critique, say AI companies should present such results?

A viral post from a biologist, subsequently endorsed by the chair and CEO of Eli Lilly, said that finding a weird cluster of genes and repeats is often the easy part, and that the hard part, where the real discoveries come from, is figuring out what the system actually does.12 Lucas Harrington, the biologist who wrote the post critiquing Anthropic's announcement, suggested that AI companies should set a high bar for what they call a discovery.34 Harrington says the aim is that when an AI actually discovers a fundamentally new biological mechanism, everyone appreciates how big a deal it is.5
