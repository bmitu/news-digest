Claude discovers novel enzyme system

3 sources · tracked since Sep 27 · single origin (Anthropic)

Anthropic announced that Claude discovered a previously unknown enzyme system hidden in the DNA of bacteriophages, the first result from its new molecular biology lab.12 Per a Latent Space summary, about 950 Claude agents used 210M tokens over 21 hours to collect more than 200k reverse transcriptases, nominate 3,500 candidate systems and prioritize 20 reports.34

Anthropic says a long array of repeating DNA sits beside the enzyme's gene, resembling CRISPR, but that it does not yet understand what the system does and that much more work is needed to learn whether it can be put to similar use.56 Early validation reportedly found the array is transcribed into distinct short RNAs, while Anthropic says the biological function and any programmable editing utility remain unknown.78

Some biologists were angered by the framing; a viral post by Lucas Harrington, reportedly endorsed by the chair and CEO of Eli Lilly, said finding a weird cluster of genes and repeats is often the easy part and the hard part is figuring out what the system does.91011 Mario Rodríguez Mestre of the University of Copenhagen said his team had already discovered the pattern, and wondered whether Anthropic learned from his Claude conversations; Anthropic denies this.1213 Zvi Mowshowitz judged that as biology it is not a big deal, comparing it to something a PhD student might find that would be cool but not headline-making.14

Commenters summarized by Latent Space were cautiously optimistic, seeing the main value as methodological, with Claude selecting a candidate and escalating it to humans, though one technical critique said the repeat-array and short-RNA analysis is fairly standard genomics.151617 MIT Technology Review's newsletter says whittling 200,000 candidates down to a few is legitimate scientific work, but that presenting AI as making discoveries itself turns the debate into breakthrough or bust.1819

What is the new enzyme system actually called, and what are its parts?

Anthropic reports that Claude-agent genome-mining workflows identified a previously uncharacterized bacteriophage system dubbed array-associated reverse transcriptases (ART).12 It has two parts: a reverse transcriptase gene plus an accessory gene, sitting next to a long CRISPR-like tandem repeat array.34 Early validation found that the array is transcribed into distinct short RNAs.56 These claims have angered some biologists. A viral post from one, subsequently endorsed by the chair and CEO of Eli Lilly, said that finding a weird cluster of genes and repeats is often the easy part, and that the hard part, where the real discoveries come from, is figuring out what the system actually does.78 Anthropic says what the system does and whether it could be used for programmable editing remain unknown.910

How does Claude's workflow narrow hundreds of thousands of candidates down to one worth testing, and how are most candidates eliminated?

Anthropic says Claude's typical workflow starts with a survey of a protein family, in which it reads the literature and reproduces established results from public data to check its methods.1 Claude then looks for family members or genomic neighbors that fit no described system, and writes a short report on each candidate that proposes a function and the evidence behind it.2 Most candidates are eliminated in the follow-up analyses, where Claude critically evaluates the evidence. Anthropic says a survey may end with a single candidate worth testing, or with none.3 Human scientists then review Claude's hypotheses and test the most promising ones, and Anthropic says all lab work is done by its scientists.4

Why does Anthropic say only a handful of known systems share these features, and what can those systems do?

Anthropic says it does not yet understand what the newly found enzyme system does, but that only a handful of known systems share its features.1 According to Anthropic, all of those known systems can cut, copy, and paste DNA.2 Anthropic says such programmable systems have historically helped revolutionize medicine, and it points to CRISPR as the foundation of genetic medicines.3 The new system's own function is unconfirmed. Anthropic says it will take much more work to learn what it does and whether it can be used the same way, and its biological function and any editing use remain unknown.456

Who did the lab work in this discovery, and how much of it did Claude do?

Anthropic says all the lab work was done by its own scientists, while Claude generated the hypotheses and candidate systems.123 By Anthropic's account, about 950 Claude agents ran for 21 hours, collected more than 200,000 reverse transcriptases, nominated 3,500 candidate systems and prioritized 20 reports.4 Commenters, as summarized by Latent Space, said Claude reportedly selected an unusual candidate, noticed an overlooked pattern, assessed novelty and escalated it for human experimental validation.56 MIT Technology Review likewise says the human scientists ran the experiments, though some biologists dispute that the result counts as a discovery.78
