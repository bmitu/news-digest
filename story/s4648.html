AI gun-detection false alarm lawsuit

Last scan Sat Nov 28, 09:22 UTC
2 sources · tracked since Nov 20 · single origin, unclear which

Four Black students, some current and some former, are being represented by the Lawyers' Committee for Civil Rights Under Law in a suit claiming they were illegally held and searched at Kenwood High School in October 2025, after Omnilert's AI weapon-detection software wrongly identified a chip bag as a gun.12 Ars Technica reports that school officials reviewing footage determined within two minutes that there was no threat and canceled the alert, yet police still confronted the students; according to the lawsuit, officers separated them, ordered them to their knees, handcuffed them and searched their persons and property.345

The Lawyers' Committee says the alert indicated a 90% probability of a gun, and that school and police officials lacked protocols to stop canceled alerts from triggering an armed response.67 Techdirt's writer says the lawsuit alleges the officers on scene distrusted Omnilert's alerts but responded anyway, quoting one officer as telling the students "AI is not the best" and another as muttering "stupid AI cameras."8 The Office of the Inspector General for Education found Omnilert's system had no bias but said Kenwood's delegation-of-authority process did not stop principal Kate Smith from texting a school resource officer to deploy police 15 minutes later, even though her text said the object was "maybe not" a gun.910 Techdirt, citing the complaint, reports that Principal Kate Smith neither recognized that the alert had been canceled nor made any effort to check if it was still in effect. School resource officer Douglas then passed the screenshot along to Baltimore County police, who responded with armed officers.1112 The OIG recommended biannual trainings, a review of school safety protocol, fewer people receiving gun alerts, and a switch to the Omnilert app.131415

Omnilert's Mitchell says the company is sorry for what the students went through but argues the school's designated staff canceled the alert within about two minutes, and that a canceled alert must stay canceled, implying the failure lay with school processes rather than the technology. inference161718 Omnilert said, in a statement Techdirt quoted, that the process "functioned as intended," putting safety and awareness first by way of quick human verification. Techdirt's author disagreed, contending that "human review" of possible false positives ought to involve more than sending a swarm of police officers to the scene.1920 The plaintiffs' side disputes that framing: the complaint alleges Omnilert markets a defective product as having "near-zero false positives" without ensuring adequate human review, and that BCPS showed deliberate indifference to students' constitutional rights.2122

Damon Hewitt of the Lawyers' Committee argues that overreliance on AI combined with poor communication among officials is a "toxic mix" and calls for a reckoning on AI use around public safety and children.2324 The suit seeks reforms to ensure public actors verify AI alerts and that false alerts do not trigger armed police responses, and Above the Law's November 20, 2026 roundup also noted the story. inference2526
