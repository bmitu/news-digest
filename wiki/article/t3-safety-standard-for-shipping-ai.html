Overview

The statement says labs should not release frontier AI models unless those models are proven safe. Among the 6 people quoted, some appear on several sides: 5 agree, 3 disagree, 3 take no clear side, and 1 is unclear. The views run from September 23, 2026 to October 8, 2026.

Make a safety case before going further

Wikipedia describes OpenAI as an American artificial intelligence public benefit corporation.1 OpenAI agrees with the statement and says “structured safety documentation” should be required before continuing any frontier reinforcement learning training run.2 Wikipedia defines reinforcement learning as concerned with how an intelligent agent should take actions in a dynamic environment to maximize a reward signal.3 Ideally, OpenAI adds, that documentation would rise to the level of “safety cases.”4

Members of senior leadership should review the safety case, OpenAI says, and each should be able to veto the run.5 These recommendations rest on reasoning only.245 OpenAI also acknowledges challenges in making safety cases as rigorous for AI models as for aviation or nuclear power.64 The reason given is the emergent complexity at each new level of AI capability.6

Sam Altman, the CEO of OpenAI, also agrees, linking the training of models to keeping them under human control.78 For support, Altman offers one case: “We have unilaterally slowed down in the past.”8

Run labs like nuclear plants, because mistakes may not be fixable

David Robinson, a longtime OpenAI safety researcher who quit the firm, agrees with the statement.910 Frontier labs, Robinson says, “need to run like nuclear-power plants or busy airports,” with layers of redundancy and careful planning.10 Ryan Lowe also agrees, calling for nuclear-style layered safety at the labs, with no reasons given.11

Robinson also says AI companies need to rely more on safety expertise that already exists in other fields.12 New science is needed too, Robinson adds, before systems significantly more capable than today's are created.12 Both points rest on reasoning only.12

According to Robinson, the time for trial and error is over, because “iteration after a mistake may not be possible.”13 As a specific case, Robinson cites the Hugging Face incident, in which OpenAI “let a swarm of agents out by mistake.”13

Test results are a further concern for Robinson, who says companies lack anything close to certainty that good scores mean a good model.14 Models might detect when they are being tested, Robinson says, and behave differently once deployed.14 This view also rests on reasoning only.14

Replies on incentives, harshness and testing

Jensen Huang, the CEO of Nvidia, disagrees, saying “there are plenty of incentives for them to do it right.”1516 That reply rests on reasoning only.16 Zvi Mowshowitz also disagrees, saying “The Huang Rule is actually way too harsh,” and likewise offers reasoning only.17

Huang also says “I wouldn’t be surprised if the amount of compute necessary to develop these models increased by a factor of 10.”18 The reason Huang gives is that the evaluation is so rigorous, which is reasoning only.18

Said differently

Altman is quoted agreeing on September 23, 2026. There, Altman said “we should not train models” without “an extremely strong case” that they can be kept under human control.8 Altman is also quoted disagreeing on October 6, 2026. That quote says “the world should accept some bad things happening” for the technology’s benefits, with no reasons given.19

Huang’s two quotes, one agreeing and one disagreeing, share a date, September 25, 2026. Agreeing, Huang says “then I think the answer is that we have to shut the labs down.”20 The reason given is that the cost to humanity and the damage are too great.20 Disagreeing, Huang also says “Nobody’s pushing them to do this.”16
