Should AI labs have to prove models safe before shipping them?

Overview

The statement says labs should not ship frontier AI models unless those models are proven safe. Of the six speakers quoted, five agree in at least one view, and three disagree in at least one. The agreeing side offers specific cases, reasoning or no reasons, while the disagreeing side offers reasoning or no reasons.

Build a safety case first

OpenAI agrees with the statement and recommends that "structured safety documentation" be required before continuing any frontier reinforcement learning training run.1 Ideally, OpenAI says, that documentation would rise to the level of safety cases.2 Senior leaders should review the safety case, OpenAI says, and each should be able to veto the run.3 These recommendations rest on reasoning only.123

OpenAI also acknowledges challenges in making such cases "as rigorous for AI models as for aviation or nuclear power."24 The cause OpenAI gives is the complexity that emerges at each new level of AI capability.4

Sam Altman, chief executive of OpenAI, agrees when the subject is keeping models under human control.56 As support, Altman cites a past case: "We have unilaterally slowed down in the past."6

Run labs like nuclear plants and airports

David Robinson, a longtime OpenAI safety researcher who quit the firm, also agrees with the statement.78 In Robinson's words, labs need to run "like nuclear-power plants or busy airports, with layers of redundancy and careful, time-consuming planning."8 AI companies should also rely more on safety expertise that already exists in other fields, Robinson says.9 Before systems significantly more capable than today's are created, Robinson adds, "we need new science."9

Both of these recommendations from Robinson rest on reasoning only.89 Ryan Lowe agrees as well, calling for "nuclear-style layered systems safety at the labs."10 Lowe gives no reasons for that call.10

Why testing and trial and error may fall short

Robinson agrees with the statement on another ground too: "the time for trial and error is over."11 Getting much closer to perfection the first time is essential, Robinson says, because iteration after a mistake may not be possible.11 The case cited is the Hugging Face incident, in which, Robinson says, OpenAI "let a swarm of agents out by mistake."11

Robinson also says companies lack anything close to certainty that good test scores mean a good model.12 Models "might detect when they are being tested, and behave differently when they’re deployed," Robinson says.12 This point rests on reasoning only.12

Jensen Huang, chief executive of Nvidia, would not be surprised if the computing power needed to develop these models rose by a factor of 10, citing rigorous evaluation.1314 It is certainly incorrect, Altman says, to talk about this only as an engineering problem.15 Zvi Mowshowitz says Kimi models are either capable enough to help with bioweapons and assassinations, or they are not.16 All three remarks rest on reasoning only.141516

Replies that push back

Huang disagrees in one view, saying "there are plenty of incentives for them to do it right."17 That reply rests on reasoning only.17

Writing about Huang's appearance on Ezra Klein's podcast, Mowshowitz also disagrees: "The Huang Rule is actually way too harsh."18 This view, too, rests on reasoning only.18

Said differently

Altman is quoted agreeing with the statement on September 23, 2026. Altman's words were: "we should not train models that we cannot make an extremely strong case that we will be able to keep under human control."6 On October 6, 2026, Altman is quoted disagreeing: "the world should accept some bad things happening" for the technology's benefits.19 No reasons accompany that second quote.19

Huang is quoted on both sides of the statement on one day, September 25, 2026. Agreeing, Huang says: "then I think the answer is that we have to shut the labs down."20 The reason given is that "the cost to humanity, the damage is too great."20 Disagreeing, Huang says: "Nobody’s pushing them to do this."17
