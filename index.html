U.S. appeals court upholds designation of Anthropic as supply chain risk
3 sources · 499 points on Hacker News · tracked since Sep 27 · 1 new article today · 2 independent origins: Ars Technica, CNBC

Judge Gregory Katsas, writing for the majority with Judge Neomi Rao, said the Department had ample support for concluding that continued use of Claude in its systems presented a statutorily covered national-security risk. Ars Technica reports the court held that no bad motive is required under 41 U.S.C. § 4713, whose definition covers the risk that "any person" may manipulate covered technology, and said the Secretary's concern about Anthropic disabling Claude from lawful actions qualifies.

The ruling does not conflict with an earlier California ruling: a Northern District of California judge ruled last month that the action was illegal under a different statute, and the appeals court said it had no quarrel with that court's conclusions on bad motive under section 3252 but that section 4713 is broader.

Judge Karen Henderson dissented, arguing the statute's verbs are directed at deliberately impeding or eavesdropping and should not cover a contractor's honest and upfront enforcement of restrictions on use that the government disfavors.

An Anthropic spokesperson said the company respectfully disagrees, noting another federal court held the parallel designation unlawful, and that it is considering all options including further review. CNBC reports the panel delayed the decision's effect so Anthropic can seek rehearing or en banc review, and Anthropic could also ask the Supreme Court to take the case. Ars Technica adds that Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick recently said the Trump administration and Anthropic have patched up their relationship and are "in tune."

Project Suncatcher TPU satellite
3 sources · tracked since Sep 27 · 1 new article today · 2 independent origins: Google, TechCrunch

Google's prototype orbital compute satellite for Project Suncatcher launched from California aboard a SpaceX rocket, which TechCrunch describes as the first time Google has sent one of its advanced chips into space. The satellite, built by Planet Labs, is meant to show that a Google TPU can work in space, and Latent Space reports it carries four TPUs on the SpaceX Transporter-18 mission.

Once commissioned, the satellite will run its TPU in 15-minute bursts to avoid straining its power and thermal systems, and Google and Planet plan a next-year demo of two more purpose-built satellites linked by laser. Google's long-term vision is an orbital data center of 81 satellites flying in close formation; TechCrunch reports that the focus of this "long-term moonshot," as Google executive Travis Beals puts it, is on building for future space infrastructure and AI workloads.

TechCrunch reports that, based on Google's analysis, a similar cost-reduction trajectory would take Starship about 1,800 launches over the next 10 years, or 180 a year, if it can fly 200 metric tons on each mission. TechCrunch calls that a big ask for a vehicle that has never flown more than five times in a year. The researchers stress that their analysis is not an economic feasibility study, while TechCrunch notes Elon Musk has suggested far higher Starship flight rates but adds that "Musk says a lot of things".

On radiation, Google says its Trillium TPUs survive a total ionizing dose greater than a five-year space mission would deliver, according to Import AI. TechCrunch adds that Google redid its particle-accelerator tests after realizing the chip configuration gave more shielding than it would get in orbit, which produced slightly more logic errors, though Google remains confident in inference workloads. Beals says the error rate is very low for typical inference, about one in a million, but was already problematic for mega-scale training runs with thousands of chips running for months.

Import AI's author calls cooling a likely challenge and says it seems very likely humanity will move a large amount of computation into orbit quickly, an opinion TechCrunch's launch-rate caveats complicate.

Nature: Stratego Superhuman AI
2 sources · tracked since Oct 1 · 1 new article today · 2 independent origins: Ars Technica, Sam Sokota

A Nature paper was flagged (in a Latent Space newsletter) as presenting the first superhuman Stratego AI, built on RL and test-time compute under imperfect information. The AI, called Ataraxos, was built by a team of researchers from Carnegie Mellon, MIT, New York University and Stanford, and Ars Technica reports it beat Pim Niemeijer, arguably the best Stratego player of all time, 15 games to one with four draws. Niemeijer played 20 online games over three weeks, and he won just once; at the 2025 Stratego World Championship, the AI won 38 of 40 games against attendees.

Ataraxos learned by self-play over 163 million games, and its key innovation over DeepMind's DeepNash was thinking ahead before each move. The team made big, bold changes in strategy early in training and small, careful ones later, because hidden information tends to send self-play learning algorithms around in circles.

Ataraxos reportedly needed 16 GPUs for a week plus four GPUs for four days, whereas DeepNash was trained for two to three months on 1,024 chips, a run the team estimates would cost $3 million to $4.5 million at 2025 prices. It played about 34 times fewer games than DeepNash, and the same approach beat three world champions at Barrage Stratego.

Researchers claim Niemeijer's single win was not really a flaw, and Gabriele Farina says that "Even a perfect strategy, sometimes it will just lose." Farina says the bot has "skewed the metagame a little bit," and Ataraxos currently cannot explain its moves.

Protein watermarking research (SynthID Bio)
2 sources · tracked since Oct 1 · 2 independent origins: Ars Technica, Google DeepMind

Latent Space's AINews roundup, citing @demishassabis, reports that SynthID Bio, watermarking for AI-generated proteins, is published in Nature, with open-sourced tools. Google DeepMind published a research paper proposing protein watermarking, a system that marks AI-designed protein sequences without compromising their function so that proteins from trusted researchers can be identified. The problem it targets is that DNA-screening software does not pick out AI-designed proteins, since nobody has characterized them well enough to know whether they are threats.

Ars Technica reports the team started with ProteinMPNN, with a SynthID variant (SynthIDBio) that uses a key and the identity of previously chosen amino acids to suggest a new one; ProteinMPNN then checks whether it works for forming a functional protein and rejects it if not, so watermark amino acids are only incorporated when consistent with a functional protein. Because the watermark is spread randomly along the protein, detection is not yes-or-no: one must scan the whole sequence with the key and measure how often the suggested amino acids appear.

In tests, watermarked proteins designed to interact with key natural proteins bound their intended targets, which Ars Technica calls less rigorous than a catalyst test but suggestive of no serious problems in harder design tasks.

Google envisions DNA synthesizers using keys from trusted organizations to quickly tell whether an unknown protein is a trusted AI design, letting them focus on untrusted ones, though this does not guarantee DNA order security. The team itself highlights several weaknesses: security depends on how keys are distributed and maintained, very short proteins may carry too few watermark amino acids, and fusing the protein with an unwatermarked one might dilute the watermark.
