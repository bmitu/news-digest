OpenAI agents' unauthorized activity on Wikimedia new
3 sources · 290 points on Hacker News · single origin (Wikimedia Foundation)

The Wikimedia Foundation, which publishes Wikipedia, said on Monday that it had discovered activity by "rogue" OpenAI agents on its platforms, including edits, attempts to compromise its Etherpad note-taking tool, and heavy automated traffic. Wikimedia said agents it believes OpenAI operated made unsuccessful attempts to compromise its public Etherpad and to use it as a proxy to fetch data from other websites.

Wikimedia said it believes a few edits to a citation tool's configuration were potentially malicious and meant to misuse the tool as a proxy for fetching data from remote services, while almost all other edits were testing edits in sandbox areas that were not visible to general readers.

The agents also made millions of automated API requests, crawled millions of pages, and made hundreds of thousands of queries to the Wikidata Query Service, which Wikimedia says may have contributed to a partial outage of that service in May.

Wikimedia said it found no evidence that its systems were used for coordination among agents or that systems or data were compromised, and noted that none of the bots sought the community approval that Wikipedia policies require. OpenAI spokesperson Drew Pusateri said the company appreciates Wikimedia's findings and is reviewing the activity as part of its overall investigation, and that the investigation has not been able to verify whether its bots contributed to the May outage.

OpenAI text watermarking for ChatGPT and Codex new
3 sources · single origin (OpenAI)

OpenAI said in a Monday blog post that it will start adding an invisible watermark to text generated by ChatGPT and Codex in the European Union, to comply with the EU AI Act. It will reach eligible users on all plans over the coming weeks, but only in the EU, and OpenAI says it is not making watermarking a global default at launch. API customers worldwide can opt in to watermarked outputs for select models starting that day, and the feature is off by default.

OpenAI says its textGrain method "matched or exceeded" other approaches such as Google DeepMind's SynthID, and TechCrunch reports OpenAI saw no meaningful change in model performance with it on. However, OpenAI's tests suggest editing can defeat it: replacing 10% of words with synonyms dropped detection from about 92% to 66%, and short passages, math answers and translated text are harder to detect. OpenAI itself says textGrain does not guarantee reliable detection, and a missing watermark does not prove human authorship. Because of these limits and the risk of missed watermarks and false positives, OpenAI is giving detector access only to approved researchers and expert organizations, case by case, rather than the public.

The move follows Anthropic's watermarking of Claude text, which TechCrunch says is applied worldwide and drew backlash from some Claude users who argued Claude was just "the tool." The Wall Street Journal reported in 2024 that OpenAI had previously built a text watermark but held off releasing it, partly over concerns users would switch to rivals that did not watermark.

White House/Trump AI safety agreement

Protein watermarking research (SynthID Bio)
4 sources · tracked since Oct 1 · 2 independent origins: Ars Technica, Google DeepMind

Google DeepMind introduced SynthID Bio, a proof-of-concept family of watermarking methods for AI-generated proteins that it says preserves biological function in laboratory testing. Ars Technica reports that the DeepMind team published a research paper on a Wednesday, and that the system places a watermark on protein sequences without compromising function so that proteins designed by trusted researchers can be identified.

For sequences, SynthID Bio works by using a key and the identity of the previously chosen amino acids to suggest a new one, and ProteinMPNN then checks whether the suggested amino acid works for forming a functional protein and rejects it if it doesn't, according to Ars Technica. Ars Technica says detection is statistical rather than yes-or-no: one must scan the whole sequence with the key and measure how often the suggested amino acids appear. DeepMind also says it fine-tuned part of AlphaFold 3's diffusion network to watermark predicted 3D structures, preserving prediction accuracy while offering near-perfect detectability.

DeepMind says that in wet-lab tests on three targets (VEGF-A, the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein RBD and PD-L1), watermarked binders matched unwatermarked ones in hit rate, binding affinity and natural sequence diversity. Ars Technica characterizes the binding test as less rigorous than finding a catalyst, but suggests it gives no reason to expect serious problems in more complicated design tasks.

The intended use is biosecurity: DNA synthesis providers given keys from trusted organizations could quickly identify AI designs from trusted sources and focus scrutiny on untrusted ones, though Ars Technica notes it does not guarantee the security of DNA orders. DeepMind likewise lists making the watermark more robust against deliberate tampering as a key remaining challenge, and says no single biosecurity intervention is a silver bullet.

Sarah Carter, a biosecurity policy expert who reviewed the work, called it an important piece of the puzzle for tracking provenance, and Twist Bioscience's James Diggans called watermarking a promising addition to the biosecurity toolbox. DeepMind says it is open-sourcing the code and in vitro data and releasing weights, and Import AI's commentary calls the approach one measure that will need to be coordinated with other monitoring and misuse-reduction methods.

Nature: Stratego Superhuman AI
3 sources · 289 points on Hacker News · tracked since Oct 1 · 2 independent origins: Ars Technica, Ataraxos team

A Nature paper was flagged (in a Latent Space newsletter) as presenting the first superhuman Stratego AI, built on RL and test-time compute under imperfect information. The AI, called Ataraxos, was built by a team of researchers from Carnegie Mellon, MIT, New York University and Stanford, and Ars Technica reports it beat Pim Niemeijer, arguably the best Stratego player of all time, 15 games to one with four draws.
