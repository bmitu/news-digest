A higher interest rate, dollar or oil price is good for some businesses and bad for others. Each cell shows how much an industry's shares moved compared with the whole US stock market, on average, in a week that indicator rose by the amount under its name. The top row is the whole market by its own price, so the two add up: in a week the VIX rose 5 points, the market fell by the top row's figure and an industry by that figure plus its own. A falling indicator reverses the sign. This is a table of past links, not a reading of today: the indicators page says where each one stands now.

Each link is measured in two five-year windows, Sep 2016 to Aug 2021 and Sep 2021 to Aug 2026, and counts as clear where it is well beyond chance (a t-statistic of 2 or more). Green or red: clear in both windows and the same way; the figure is the last five years'. Light green or light red, leaning good or bad: the same way in both windows but clear in only one. Mixed: the two windows point opposite ways and at least one is clear, so the link has changed (for the whole market, rising 10-year yields went with rising shares before 2021 and with falling ones since 2022, when inflation took over). No clear effect: clear in neither. These are averages over many weeks; any one week also moves with earnings, news and the other indicators, which move together, so one cell can carry another's effect. Not a forecast and not advice. The mortgage rate is not shown: Freddie Mac's is a weekly survey of lenders that trails market rates, too slow to line up with weekly share prices; the 10-year yield is the nearer guide. Of the 90 industry cells: 8 good, 9 bad, 10 leaning good, 18 leaning bad, 16 mixed, 29 no clear effect.

The industries

Kenneth R. French's industry portfolios: every US-listed company's shares grouped by its SIC industry code, weighted by market value. His groupings and names are used rather than the familiar GICS sectors, which they do not match exactly (his Utilities include gas pipelines, which GICS counts as energy; his Real Estate leaves out REITs).
Banking
Commercial banks, savings institutions, credit unions, consumer and business lenders, mortgage bankers, Fannie Mae
Insurance
Life, health, property and casualty, title insurers and insurance brokers
Real Estate
Property owners, operators, agents and developers; not REITs, which French counts with trading firms
Construction
Homebuilders, general and heavy-construction contractors
Retail
Stores of every kind, car dealers and gas stations
Restaurants, Hotels, Motels
Restaurants, hotels and other lodging
Automobiles and Trucks
Car, truck and parts makers, tires, trailers and motor homes
Aircraft
Aircraft, engine and parts makers
Transportation
Airlines, railroads, trucking, shipping, freight forwarding and oil pipelines
Petroleum and Natural Gas
Oil and gas producers, drillers, field services and refiners
Utilities
Electric, gas and water utilities and natural gas pipelines
Precious Metals
Gold and silver miners
Computer Software
Software, programming, data processing and information services
Electronic Equipment
Chips and electronic components, communications equipment
Pharmaceutical Products
Drug and biotech makers, diagnostics
