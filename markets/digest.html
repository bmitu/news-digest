Fed bank supervision restructuring and asset thresholds new
3 sources · single origin (Federal Reserve)

Federal Reserve Vice Chair for Supervision Michelle Bowman announced on Tuesday, October 6, that the Fed will restructure bank supervision, replacing a model in which regional bank presidents oversaw examinations with one more accountable to Washington, according to Reuters. Each of the five regions will have a regional leader accountable for all supervisory activity, still conducted by regional Reserve Bank staff, and Bowman says the regions will follow state lines rather than Reserve Bank district boundaries. Bowman says examiners will remain in existing Reserve Bank locations and keep overseeing the banks they currently supervise.

Bowman argues the current structure "disincentivized a critical link between responsibility and accountability," citing an independent review of Silicon Valley Bank's collapse that she commissioned and that found Fed examiners were slow to act. She also criticized the Fed's heavy use of committees, saying they "became a source for plausible deniability and a disincentive for examiners to take prompt and decisive action," and argued their use should be streamlined.

Bowman also said the Fed will consider later this year updating fixed-dollar asset thresholds that determine when banks face stricter rules, with a mechanism to update them every five years for inflation and economic growth. Reuters reports the plan could give banks more room to grow before triggering new capital, liquidity and stress testing rules, and that banks have complained for years that fixed levels become outdated. In her speech, Bowman said a community bank has been defined for 15 years as one with under $10 billion in assets, and that the Board will consider broader reforms and updates to the large bank tailoring framework later this year.

US employers added just 29,000 jobs in September 2026, well below expectations, and the unemployment rate rose to 4.2%, the Bureau of Labor Statistics reported on Friday. Private employers added 46,000 jobs while governments shed 17,000, according to Wolf Richter. Healthcare drove most of the gains, adding 17,000 jobs per CNBC, while information lost 10,000 and financial activities 7,000.

Revisions removed a combined 60,000 jobs from earlier months: August was revised to a gain of 133,000 and July to a loss of 10,000 (per CNBC). Oil companies' Supreme Court climate lawsuit appeal
2 sources · tracked since Oct 5 · 1 new article today · single origin (Investing.com)

The US Supreme Court opened its new nine-month term on Monday, Oct 5, by hearing a bid by ExxonMobil and Suncor Energy to thwart a climate-damages lawsuit brought by officials in Boulder, Colorado. The companies appealed after the Colorado Supreme Court let Boulder's lawsuit, which accuses them of state law violations, proceed. Boulder's city and county governments accuse the companies of helping drive climate change and misleading the public about fossil fuel risks, and aim to hold them liable for past and future costs such as infrastructure repairs, environmental damage, emergency management and public health harms.

Exxon and Suncor lawyer Kannon Shanmugam told the justices the case is an unprecedented effort to use state law to regulate global conduct, and argued that federal law governs interstate pollution claims. The companies also argue that Boulder's claims intrude on an area controlled by the federal government as well as the Clean Air Act. President Donald Trump's administration has backed the companies, arguing that the federal government's authority to regulate air pollution precludes Boulder's claims. Backers of the oil companies argue that allowing Boulder's most far-reaching claims could give states sweeping power over conduct far outside their borders.

Exxon and Suncor told the justices that nearly 60 state and local governments have brought similar suits seeking billions of dollars, and a ruling for the companies could lead to many of those cases being dismissed. Energy companies and allied states have built a largely winning record before the justices over the past two decades in climate liability and federal environmental regulation cases. The court has a 6-3 conservative majority; Justice Samuel Alito has recused himself, and he owns stock in several oil and gas companies but not Exxon or Suncor, according to his financial disclosure forms. A decision is expected by the end of June.
