Aggregate Bond Index first negative 5-year returns

Last scan Sun Oct 4, 19:31 UTC
2 sources · single origin (Ben Carlson)

Ben Carlson of Wealth of Common Sense writes that the Bloomberg Aggregate Bond Index is in its first cycle of negative rolling 5-year returns since the index's 1976 inception date.12 Barry Ritholtz's morning reads roundup for The Big Picture highlighted Carlson's post and repeated that finding.3

Carlson says rolling 10-year returns are still positive, though bond investors came close to a lost decade during the 2022 bear market.4 He says that after inflation the picture is worse: the only time 5-year real returns were worse was in the early 1980s, and current 10-year real returns are as bad as they have ever been.56

Carlson attributes the poor results to low starting yields, interest rates that rose quickly, and high inflation.7 He adds that returns have even underperformed what starting yields would have implied this decade.8

Carlson calls it the most brutal bond market in modern finance history, but says the average yield to maturity for the Agg is now 5.5%, which is good for forward returns.910 He says he has not met anyone able to predict interest rate moves, and argues investors should weigh bond types by yield, credit quality, duration and maturity rather than forecast rates.1112

How did the Agg's 2022 loss compare with its worst years in the late 1970s and early 1980s?

The Bloomberg Aggregate Bond Index lost 13% in 2022, which Ben Carlson says was its first double-digit down year ever.1 By contrast, Carlson says the index had no down year at all from 1976 to 1981, and its weakest late-1970s showing was a 1.4% gain in 1978.23 So the 2022 loss was a double-digit decline, while the worst late-1970s year was still a gain, though Carlson says the late-1970s figures are nominal and that inflation and yields were much higher then. inference14 Carlson adds that the only time 5-year real returns were worse than the current cycle was in the early 1980s, when double-digit inflation was running.56 Carlson says there are a few reasons today's bond market is even worse than what investors experienced in the late-1970s and early-1980s.7

Ben Carlson says cash yields are back up to 4%, while U.S. government bonds yield more than 5%.1 He puts the Agg at approaching 6%, with corporate bonds yielding more than 6%.2 Yields therefore rise with risk, from cash at the bottom to corporates at the top, and Carlson notes that even higher yields are available by taking more risk. inference34 Carlson gives the Agg's yield as approaching 6% in one passage, while elsewhere he gives the Agg's average yield to maturity as 5.5%.56

What could push interest rates and bond returns up or down from here, according to Carlson?

Carlson says higher rates would bring more short-term price pain, but higher starting yields can offset it and should raise forward return expectations.12 In the other direction, Carlson says rates could keep rising if economic growth stays strong, AI spending grows even bigger, or the Iran war drags on.3 He adds that the war in Iran could come to an end and energy prices could fall.4 Carlson doesn't think anyone can predict these moves, including the Fed, so he advises investors to weigh yield, credit quality, duration and maturity instead of forecasting.56
