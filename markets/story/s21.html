Bond market first negative 5-year return

Last scan Sun Oct 4, 16:14 UTC
2 sources · tracked since Oct 3 · single origin (Ben Carlson)

Ben Carlson of Wealth of Common Sense wrote that the Bloomberg Aggregate Bond Index has, for the first time in its history, posted negative rolling 5-year returns, measured through the end of August.12 Barry Ritholtz of The Big Picture highlighted Carlson's piece in his Wednesday morning reading list, repeating the first-negative-5-year-return finding.3

Carlson says 10-year returns are still positive, though bond investors came close to a lost decade during the 2022 bear market.4 After inflation, Carlson says, the only time 5-year real returns were worse was in the early 1980s, when inflation ran in the double digits, and current 10-year real returns are as bad as they have ever been.56 He notes the Agg fell 13% in 2022, its first double-digit down year, and that 2021 (-1.5%) and 2022 were its first back-to-back down years, with a drawdown of nearly 20%, the worst in its history.789

Carlson attributes the poor results to low starting yields, a fast rise in interest rates and high inflation.10 He calls this the most brutal bond market in modern finance history, though he adds that the Agg's average yield to maturity is now 5.5%, which he says is good for forward returns.1112 Carlson cautions that no one can reliably predict interest rate moves, and he advises investors to weigh bond types by yield, credit quality, duration and maturity, adding that higher rates could bring short-term price pain that higher starting yields can offset.131415

How does today's bond bear market compare with the late 1970s and early 1980s, when the index had no down years?

Ben Carlson argues today's bond market is even worse than what investors experienced in the late-1970s and early-1980s, calling it the most brutal in modern finance history.123 The Bloomberg Aggregate had no down year from 1976 to 1981, and its worst return in that stretch was a 1.4% gain in 1978.45 Today's cycle has been far harsher: the Agg fell 13% in 2022 for its first double-digit down year, and 2021 and 2022 were its first back-to-back down years.67 The bear market that began in 2020 also produced the Agg's worst drawdown ever, a decline of nearly 20%.8 This cycle also marks the first negative 5-year returns in the index's history, while the only worse 5-year real returns came in the early 1980s, when inflation ran in the double digits.910 Carlson says the 1970s returns were nominal and inflation was much higher then, but yields were also much higher. He blames today's poor results on low starting yields, a rapid rise in rates and high inflation.1112

How much have yields on cash, Treasuries and corporate bonds risen, and what do they pay now compared with the Agg's 5.5%?

Ben Carlson says cash yields are back up to 4%, U.S. government bonds yield more than 5%, and corporate bonds yield more than 6%.12 Carlson puts the Agg's average yield to maturity at 5.5% in one passage, and elsewhere says the Agg is approaching 6%.34 Compared with the 5.5% Agg yield, Treasuries at just over 5% pay slightly less, and corporates at over 6% pay more, while cash at 4% pays clearly less. inference352 Wolf Richter says the bond bear market started in August 2020, and that six years into it the 10-year yield has risen from 0.5% to 5.28%. He adds that the 30-year Treasury yield closed the week at 5.63%.67 However, it is not said how much cash or corporate bond yields have risen over the same period; it is said only that cash is back to 4% and that yields overall are at levels not seen in almost 20 years.

What could push rates lower or higher from here, and what happens to bond returns in each case?

Ben Carlson lists several things that could push rates lower: falling inflation, slower economic growth, a recession, an AI-trade collapse, or an end to the war in Iran that brings energy prices down.1 He says rates could also keep rising if growth stays strong, AI spending grows even bigger, or the Iran war drags on.2 If rates rise, Carlson expects some more short-term price pain. He argues that higher starting yields can now offset that pain, and that expected forward returns rise as rates go higher.3 Carlson also notes that the Agg's average yield to maturity is 5.5%, which he calls a good thing for forward returns. He adds that he has yet to encounter anyone who can predict the direction or magnitude of interest rate moves, so he says investors shouldn't try to predict them and should weigh bond types by yield, credit quality, duration and maturity.45
