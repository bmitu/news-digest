30-year Treasury yield highest since 2002

Last scan Sun Oct 4, 17:18 UTC
5 sources · 4 independent origins: Barron's, Bespoke Investment Group, Financial Times, Wolf Street

The 30-year Treasury yield briefly hit 5.69% on Thursday, the highest since 2002, according to Wolf Richter, and closed the week at 5.63%.1 Bespoke said the 30-year yield had climbed above 5.6%, its highest level in over 24 years, and that if the yield finished higher that day it would be the sixth consecutive session with a higher 30-year yield.23 Bespoke also noted that the 30-year yield sits 3.3 standard deviations above its 50-day moving average, the most extended level since May.4 The Financial Times headline, as quoted by Barry Ritholtz, says US long-term borrowing costs touched their highest level since 2004, with 30-year Treasury yields at a two-decade high.5

Richter said the 10-year yield closed the week at 5.28%, up 11 basis points on the week, after swings including a drop to 5.15% on Friday's jobs headlines.678

The Financial Times cites an oil-price jump, rising inflation expectations and another weak buyback operation.9 Paul Krugman argues the immediate cause of the latest spike was Donald Trump's rejection of an Iranian proposal to end the war, which pushed oil prices back up and, he says, keeps inflation and Federal Reserve rates high.1011

Richter says that at 5.6% the 30-year is not at a 'blood-in-the-street' level but a fairly normal yield after 14 years of rate repression, while a link roundup cites a view that rates are reverting to historical norms. inference1213

How much is the 30-year Treasury auction-bought bond from 2020 or 2021 worth now, and how big are the losses for people who locked in low yields?

Wolf Richter says investors who bought 30-year bonds at Treasury auctions in the summer of 2020 now face a decline in market value of over 50%.1 As a concrete example, the Treasury paid 50.6 cents on the dollar at its September 24 buyback for a 30-year bond sold in November 2021 with a 1.875% coupon and a 1.94% yield.23 Bonds sold at auction in the summer of 2020 trade for even less than that 50.6-cent price, according to Richter.4 The losses come as the 30-year Treasury yield closed the week at 5.63%, after briefly touching 5.69%, the highest since 2002.5 Richter notes that holders who keep such a bond to maturity would still get face value back, so the paper loss is not realized unless they sell. inference6

Why did bond investors stop buying after Friday's jobs report instead of repeating the 2023 rally?

On Friday morning the 10-year yield dropped to 5.15% as the jobs headlines came out, and it looked as though the heavy buying of October 2023 might be starting again.1 Wolf Richter says investors then looked past the headlines and found the jobs data was fairly decent, with private employers adding 46,000 jobs while governments shed 17,000.2 Richter says they also remembered the problems still hanging over the bond market: the deficit and the flood of new bond supply, debt racing toward $41 trillion, stubborn inflation, competition from higher-yielding AI bonds, and a hot economy.34 As a result the rally fizzled: the 10-year yield climbed back to 5.30% in the afternoon and settled at 5.28%, up 11 basis points on the week, whereas in 2023 buying had pushed it down to 3.79% by the end of December.567 The wider backdrop was a global bond sell-off driven by fears of inflation from high oil prices, which is consistent with investors declining to chase the dip. inference89

How does the rise in the 30-year yield compare with past six-day streaks, and how unusual is the 32 basis point jump?

Bespoke Investment Group says the 30-year yield's 32 basis point rise over six days is above average compared with the first six days of past streaks of six or more straight gains.1 It is the largest such move since October 2022, when yields rose 34.5 basis points. That streak went on to eight straight sessions, which tied for the third-longest run of rising 30-year yields.23 Measured against all six-day changes, the jump is the largest since April 2025. It ranks in the 97th percentile of all six-day changes since the data began in 1977.4
