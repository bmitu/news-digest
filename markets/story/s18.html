10-year Treasury yield above 5.3%

Last scan Sun Oct 4, 03:32 UTC
3 sources · 2 independent origins: Bespoke Investment Group, The Guardian

The 10-year US Treasury yield reached 5.34% on Thursday, its highest level since 2002, according to The Guardian, as a global bond sell-off intensified.12 Barry Ritholtz likewise puts the 10-year Treasury at 5.3%, noting yields have climbed back over 5%.3 Bespoke reported that the 10-year yield finished September at 5.28%, the highest month-end level since June 12, 2007, after rising 53.5 basis points over the month.4 Bespoke says that gain ranks in the 95th percentile of all months since 1962 and that yields rose on 16 of 21 trading days, the highest share of up-days in any month since April 1981.56

The Guardian says investors are spooked by the threat of renewed inflation from persistently high oil prices, with central banks expected to raise rates in coming months.7 The sell-off came even though US inflation data on Wednesday was lower than forecast, which had been expected to calm investors.8 Axel Rudolph of IG says the data reduced expectations of an October Fed rate hike, but investors fear persistent inflation and higher oil could keep rates elevated, and a December hike prospect keeps pressure on bonds.910

Jefferies economist Mohit Kumar cites inflation, deficit and issuance concerns, and describes a buyers' strike in which investors will not step in until there is some stability.11 The sell-off spread to stocks and other bond markets: the FTSE 100 fell almost 1.7%, UK 30-year yields hit 6%, and French 10-year yields reached 4.96%.121314

What happened to the 10-year yield after the weak September jobs report, and why didn't buyers rush in as they did in October 2023?

The September jobs report was expected to show a gain of 84,000 jobs. inference1 The 10-year Treasury yield fell to 5.15% on Friday morning when the jobs headlines came out, after hitting 5.36% intraday on Thursday, and it closed the week at 5.28%.23 The drop initially looked like a repeat of October 23, 2023, when the 10-year yield hit 5% intraday, then plunged amid heavy buying and kept falling to 3.79% at the end of December.45 Other reasons for hesitancy were also reported: Jefferies' Mohit Kumar described a "buyers' strike" in which investors would not step in until there was some stability, and he cited inflation, deficit and issuance concerns.67 Richter also argues that inflation will remain a major issue, and he says 5.3% is not a "blood in the streets" level for the 10-year yield.8910 The full details of what investors found in the jobs data were not reported.
