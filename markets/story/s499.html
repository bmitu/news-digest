Euro falls to multi-month low

Last scan Mon Oct 5, 15:38 UTC
4 sources · 2 independent origins: Investing.com, The Guardian

The euro fell to a 17-month low against the dollar on Monday, with the Guardian reporting it dropped as much as 0.8% to below $1.12, its lowest since May 2025.1 Reuters reports the euro hit as low as $1.1161 in Asian hours and was last down 0.47% at $1.12, after a fourth straight weekly fall against the dollar.23 Reuters also says the dollar index rose 0.30% to 102.23 after reaching 102.53, its highest since April 10, 2025, and that analysts said the euro's appeal as the main alternative to the dollar was already fading after the Federal Reserve's September rate hike.45

Both sources link the slide mainly to fears over France's ability to control its budget deficit and a sharp bond selloff last week that raised worries of a return of sovereign debt crisis dynamics. inference67 The French-German 10-year yield gap widened last week to its highest since the eurozone debt crisis, though the sources give different figures: the Guardian says the widest since 2012, while Reuters says about 150 basis points, the highest since 2011. inference89 The Guardian also says Spanish Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez's announcement of a snap election has added to eurozone uncertainty.1011

Hauke Siemssen of Commerzbank says bond market dynamics are "increasingly concerning and somewhat reminiscent of a sovereign debt crisis", and that the French spread selloff "seems to increasingly feed on itself".1213 Roberto Mialich of UniCredit says investors do not rule out a further euro decline, making a retest of $1.10 possible in the near term.14 Francesco Pesole of ING says euro/Swiss franc is historically the cleanest hedge of eurozone fiscal risk and could fall further, though he cautions the franc's own fragilities may get in the way.1516

Why is the French budget deficit so hard to fix, and what is the government's plan to cut it?

The government of Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu has announced a €54bn savings drive that involves cutting pensions spending and funding for government departments, excluding defence.12 Lecornu says the savings would cut the deficit from 5.5% of GDP this year to 5% next year, and he warns it could reach 6.5% without action.345 Analysts argued that France's fiscal problems were daunting enough on their own, but an upcoming presidential election and a hung parliament where compromise has often proved impossible make them even harder to address.6 Planned cuts are also unpopular, as they have deepened a funding crunch in education and sparked protests across the country.78 Saxo strategist Neil Wilson notes that French government plans to reduce the budget deficit still face parliamentary scrutiny and could ultimately be watered down, and investors fear political pressures could derail fiscal consolidation.91011
