Data center construction spending report

Last scan Sun Oct 4, 16:14 UTC
2 sources · tracked since Oct 3 · single origin, unclear which

Wolf Richter of Wolf Street reports that, according to Census Bureau construction data, spending on data center construction rose 7.5% in August from July and 73% year-over-year to a seasonally adjusted annual rate of $85 billion.1 Richter says the annual rate of data center construction spending has risen 823% since the beginning of 2021.2 Richter notes that the figures cover only buildings, site improvements and integrated equipment such as HVAC, and exclude servers, racks, networking and electrical equipment, which he calls the expensive part.34

Power sector construction spending rose 0.8% in August from July and 8.5% year-over-year to an annual rate of $186 billion, up 58% since the beginning of 2021.56 Factory construction spending, at a $168 billion annual rate, was unchanged from July and down 19.8% year-over-year, yet remains nearly double data center spending, according to Richter.78

Bespoke Investment Group likewise says data centers remain an extremely big driver of construction spending growth, offsetting weakness elsewhere in nonresidential construction.9 Richter argues the buildout continues on a near-exponential curve despite efforts by people, municipalities and state governments to slow or block it, and says data centers have entered midterm election rhetoric.1011

Commenters on his site are divided: one warns that factory capacity serving data centers could be idled if construction falls off, while another attributes the rush to fear of missing out.1213

How much of a data center's total cost does the construction figure actually represent, and how much is servers and other hardware?

Census Bureau data show data center construction spending at a seasonally adjusted annual rate of $85 billion in August, but Wolf Richter says that covers only the buildings, surrounding improvements and integrated equipment such as HVAC.12 Richter calls this "the cheap part of a data center" and says the expensive part, which is not counted, is the servers, racks, networking and optical gear, and the electrical equipment that powers them.34 Economist Van Nieuwerburgh, as cited by Paul Krugman, puts numbers on the split for a multi-billion-dollar AI training campus: about one-third of the investment goes to the facility and power infrastructure, and roughly two-thirds to compute hardware and related IT systems.5

How does the growth in data center construction compare with the rest of nonresidential construction, which Krugman says has basically flatlined?

What happens to factory capacity and suppliers like Caterpillar and Cummins if hyperscalers back off because data centers are waiting years for power?

A commenter named Cole argues that if data center construction falls off, a lot of factory capacity would be idled, especially in construction equipment and backup generation, naming Caterpillar and Cummins.1 Cole's trigger is that hyperscalers will eventually back off when hundreds of data centers are left waiting years for power.2 The exposure is real in the data: data center construction spending reached an $85 billion annual rate in August, up 73% from a year earlier, and it has sent demand shockwaves through big parts of industrial manufacturing.34 Factory construction spending, at a $168 billion annual rate, is still nearly double data center spending, though it is down 19.8% from a year earlier, so a pullback by hyperscalers would hit a sector that is already off its recent peak. inference56 The article does not report any actual hyperscaler pullback or any Caterpillar or Cummins results, so the outcome for those companies remains a commenter's speculation.
