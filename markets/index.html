30-year Treasury yield hits highest since 2002 new
4 sources · 4 independent origins: Barron's, Bespoke Investment Group, Financial Times, Wolf Street

The 30-year Treasury yield briefly hit 5.69% on Thursday, which Wolf Richter says was the highest since 2002, before closing the week at 5.63%. Bespoke reported the 30-year yield climbing above 5.6%, its highest level in over 24 years, and Barron's, as linked by Tadas Viskanta, said it was back to 2002 levels. The Financial Times, as cited by Barry Ritholtz, put the level as the highest since 2004 and said the rout deepened on an oil-price jump, rising inflation expectations and another weak buyback operation.

Bespoke says the 30-year yield was 3.3 standard deviations above its 50-day moving average, the most extended since May, and its 14-day RSI reached 75, the highest since January 2025.

Richter says the 10-year yield closed the week at 5.28%, up 11 basis points, after hitting 5.36% intraday on Thursday and dropping to 5.15% on Friday's jobs data before rebounding.

Richter argues that yields of about 5.6% on the 30-year and 5.3% on the 10-year are not at blood-in-the-street levels but are just sort of normal after 14 years of interest rate repression. Richter says he is sniffing around maturities in the 3-7-year range, and that if he nibbles it will also be too early because he expects yields in that range to rise further.

September 2026 US jobs report new
7 sources · 3 independent origins: Bureau of Labor Statistics, CNBC, New Deal democrat

US employers added just 29,000 jobs in September, and the unemployment rate rose to 4.2%, the Bureau of Labor Statistics reported on Friday, far below the 84,000 gain economists surveyed by Dow Jones had expected. Private-sector employers added 46,000 jobs while governments shed 17,000, according to Wolf Richter. The previous two months were revised down: August to a gain of 133,000 and July to a loss of 10,000, together 60,000 fewer jobs than first reported. Sources differ on the details: the Guardian says economists expected just under 70,000 jobs, and it puts the August gain at 162,000, while CNBC and the BBC cite 133,000 for August after revisions.

Average hourly earnings rose 0.1% in September, putting the 12-month gain at 3%, which CNBC describes as the lowest since May 2021; Wall Street had looked for 0.3% and 3.1%. The household survey was stronger than the payroll survey: CNBC reports household employment rose by 406,000 and the labor force by 485,000.

Employ America characterized the report as "soft, but not dire", saying the prime-age employment and unemployment rates are healthy, and said the labor market is not a source of inflationary pressure. Thomas Simons of Jefferies said the number should be "the nail in the coffin for an October hike", while Heather Long of Navy Federal Credit Union called the market "stable" and does not think the Fed will be dissuaded from hiking in December.

Market-implied odds of the Fed holding at its Oct. 27-28 meeting jumped to 82.8%, according to CME's FedWatch tool, and Treasury yields slumped after the report. Wolf Richter notes the 10-year yield dropped to 5.15% on Friday morning but re-climbed and closed the week at 5.28%, as bond investors found the jobs data "pretty decent".

The BBC says the report contrasts with President Donald Trump's insistence that the economy is booming, and notes an AP/NORC poll showing 26% approve of his handling of the economy.

Anthropic IPO filing / S-1 new
4 sources · 3 independent origins: Reuters, The Ringer, Tomasz Tunguz

Anthropic's IPO prospectus, leaked to Reuters, reportedly shows a 2025 net loss of $42 billion on $4.6 billion in revenues, as the company targets an IPO valuation of $2 trillion. Wolf Richter says the net loss was five times the $8 billion of 2024 and revenues rose twelvefold, with total operating expenses of $12.6 billion including $7.3 billion on compute and infrastructure. The company held $20.3 billion in cash and short-term investments at the end of 2025, before a $65 billion Series H round in May 2026 that valued it at $965 billion. According to Reuters, the prospectus says Anthropic plans to spend $518 billion on cloud, computing and infrastructure obligations in coming years. UK diesel hits £2 a litre; G7 100 million barrel reserve release; Trump diesel export ban threat new
5 sources · 2 independent origins: BBC, The Guardian

In the UK, the RAC said average diesel prices passed £2 a litre for the first time, reaching 200.01p, compared with 142.38p before the war. The RAC says filling an average family diesel car now costs £110, almost £32 more than at the start of the US-Iran war, and its policy head Simon Williams called £2 a "price threshold no one wanted to cross".

The G7 announced on Friday 2 October 2026 that it would release 100 million barrels of oil and diesel from reserves, after Donald Trump had threatened to ban US diesel exports. The release is to be coordinated through the IEA, begin immediately and run over four months, with a frontloaded diesel release within the first 20 days. The Guardian reports, citing proposals reportedly drawn up by Emmanuel Macron, that the total is split into 50 million barrels of diesel and 50 million of crude oil, while the BBC says it is a mix of diesel and crude and that it is not yet clear which partner countries will release stocks.

G7 members also agreed there would be no export restrictions on energy products between them, and Macron said "President Trump, in particular, was very clear on this point". Accounts differ on Trump's stance: he had been considering a ban, but later said at the White House that an export ban was "never really on the table" and praised Europe's move.

Brent crude briefly fell below $100 on the news but rose back to around $102 by Friday evening; Kpler's Matt Smith said it reversed course on rumours of Saudi Arabia planning an offensive into Yemen, and also said oil had risen again due to renewed strikes between Saudi Arabia and the Houthis.

August PCE inflation release and revisions new
4 sources · 2 independent origins: Employ America, Wolf Street

The Commerce Department's Bureau of Economic Analysis reported that the PCE price index rose 0.3% in August for a 12-month gain of 3.4%, and that core PCE (excluding food and energy) rose 0.2% for an annual rate of 3%, both below what economists surveyed by Dow Jones expected.

The release coincided with annual revisions in which the BEA changed how it computes prices for legal services, software and computer accessories, and portfolio management. CNBC reports the revisions lowered the core July PCE level by 0.36 percentage point. Wolf Richter calculates that July's year-over-year core PCE was cut by 37 basis points to 2.98% from the originally reported 3.35%, and that the revisions cut July's all-items PCE increase by 34 basis points from the originally reported 3.70%.

Richter says the revised core PCE of 3.01% and all-items PCE of 3.4% remain substantially above the Fed's 2% target, and he points to a 12.3% revised July rate for computer software and accessories versus 21.2% under the old method. Employ America also notes the 3-month annualized core rate is about 2% but leans on soft June and July readings, and calls it a noisy gauge.

Markets reacted by pricing less chance of an October Fed hike; CNBC says traders pushed the next expected increase to December, while the Guardian, citing LSEG, says the October odds fell to about 35% from about 45%.

Commentators stressed that inflation is still high: Sonu Varghese of Carson Group said PCE inflation "is still running hot however you cut it," and Heather Long of Navy Federal Credit Union said the data "show no progress in August on inflation." David Russell of TradeStation said the report bolsters the case for not hiking in October but noted it is relatively old data that doesn't reflect this month's surge in diesel prices.

30-year US mortgage rates rise above 7.3% new
3 sources · single origin (Mortgage Bankers Association)

The average 30-year fixed mortgage rate has climbed to 7.30% after six straight weekly increases, according to the Mortgage Bankers Association, the highest level since late 2023. Paul Krugman writes that the rate was about 6 percent on the eve of the Iran war and was almost 7.3 percent as of last week. Krugman calculates that on a $500,000 mortgage the increase means an additional $431 in monthly payments and an additional $155,000 in interest over the life of the loan.

Krugman says the rise was especially rapid in September and has been widely described in the financial media as a "bond market rout". Krugman says the current evidence does not support the view that rising long-term rates are driven by fears of a U.S. government default or concerns about runaway inflation, and says it is likely that the biggest source of the surge is the immense demand for funds created by the AI boom. He adds that the Iran war appears to have triggered a reset of market narratives about Federal Reserve policy, and that there are signs of a balance-sheet deleveraging spiral also pushing long rates up.

CNBC's Diana Olick reports that the high rates have deterred potential homebuyers, who are also facing higher house prices than a year ago. The Guardian reports that in Salinas, California, borrowing costs are worsening a housing crisis, with mortgage rates above 7% described as a 20-month high and the Federal Reserve said to be poised to raise rates further.

Conference Board consumer confidence and JOLTS job openings new
2 sources · single origin (Conference Board)

The Conference Board reported on Tuesday that its Consumer Confidence Index fell 6.7 points to 81.9 in September, its lowest level since 2014 and well below the Dow Jones consensus forecast of 89. Respondents cited concerns over inflation and the jobs outlook, and for the first time in the four-year history of the survey question, more said their personal finances were bad than good. The Present Situation Index fell 7.9 points to 109.3 and the Expectations Index slipped 5.9 points to 63.6, while the gap between those saying jobs are "plentiful" and "hard to get" narrowed by 2.5 percentage points to 1.7%.

Average expected inflation rose 0.3 percentage point to 6.1%, and the median rose 0.3 point to 5.1%, with CNBC linking this to uncertainty over the Iran war and surging Treasury yields and mortgage rates. Conference Board chief economist Dana Peterson said consumer appraisals of current business conditions turned negative for the first time since September 2024, and that references to prices, and oil and gas prices in particular, rose to new heights, reflecting September's surge in fuel costs. CNBC noted the Conference Board reading is in keeping with similar surveys, as the University of Michigan survey showed sentiment fell 7% in September to its second-lowest reading on record.

The Bureau of Labor Statistics reported that August job openings edged lower by 256,000 to 7.08 million, below the Wall Street consensus of 7.2 million, with sharp declines in professional and business services and healthcare-related jobs. Bespoke Investment Group, by contrast, said job openings "continue to improve" and described a stark disconnect between hard employment data and consumers' evaluations of the labor market, a framing that differs from CNBC's report of a decline in openings.

Aggregate Bond Index first negative 5-year returns new
2 sources · single origin (Ben Carlson)

Ben Carlson of Wealth of Common Sense writes that the Bloomberg Aggregate Bond Index is in its first cycle of negative rolling 5-year returns since the index's 1976 inception date. Barry Ritholtz's morning reads roundup for The Big Picture highlighted Carlson's post and repeated that finding.

Carlson says rolling 10-year returns are still positive, though bond investors came close to a lost decade during the 2022 bear market. He says that after inflation the picture is worse: the only time 5-year real returns were worse was in the early 1980s, and current 10-year real returns are as bad as they have ever been.

Carlson attributes the poor results to low starting yields, interest rates that rose quickly, and high inflation. He adds that returns have even underperformed what starting yields would have implied this decade.

Carlson calls it the most brutal bond market in modern finance history, but says the average yield to maturity for the Agg is now 5.5%, which is good for forward returns. He says he has not met anyone able to predict interest rate moves, and argues investors should weigh bond types by yield, credit quality, duration and maturity rather than forecast rates.
